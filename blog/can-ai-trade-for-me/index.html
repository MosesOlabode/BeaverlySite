AI + markets

Can AI trade for me?

Yes, software can carry out financial-market activity for you through a connected account. The harder question is what you actually want to delegate — and how much authority the software should have.

September 12, 20267 min read

When someone asks “Can AI trade for me?”, they are usually asking for more than market predictions. They want to know whether software can take over the repetitive work: watch supported markets, decide when the conditions fit, carry out an action through an account, keep working while they are away, and explain what happened later.

That is possible today. But products that sound similar can be doing very different things underneath. A fixed trading bot, a copy-trading service, a signal assistant, a discretionary managed account and an AI-driven automation product are not the same system.

The word AI tells you surprisingly little about which one you are getting.

First decide what “trade for me” means

There are several levels of delegation:

  • Advice only: software suggests an action and you decide whether to place it.
  • Signals: a service tells you when something has happened or when a setup may exist.
  • Copy trading: your account mirrors another trader or strategy according to the provider’s rules.
  • Rule-based automation: a bot follows a predefined set of conditions and actions.
  • Goal-led automation: you state what you are working towards, review the material plan, authorize it, and software carries out ongoing work inside that approved scope.

Those models can all be useful. The right one depends on how much control you want to keep, how much setup you want to do yourself, and who you want making the ongoing decisions.

The most important question is authority

An AI can be excellent at understanding language and still be the wrong place to store financial authority.

A conversational model may misunderstand a sentence, hallucinate a state, or become unavailable. If every sentence it produces can directly become a live market action, the system has coupled intelligence and authority too tightly.

At Beaverly, we separate them. Chilla can understand the goal, reason about supported product choices, prepare work and explain what happened. Live execution goes through M-II, Beaverly’s financial execution system. Authorization, account scope, sizing and comfort controls, provider state and execution truth are enforced outside the language model.

An AI can help decide what to propose without being allowed to invent what you approved.

That separation is one of the first things worth checking in any product that claims it can “trade for you.”

Does the platform hold your money?

Another major distinction is custody.

Some financial products require you to send funds to the product itself. Others connect to an account you already have with an external provider. Those are different trust models.

Chilla is non-custodial. Beaverly does not become the place where your market funds are stored. Live work happens through a supported account you choose to connect, while the funds remain with that provider.

Non-custody does not remove market risk. It simply keeps the job of holding funds separate from the job of providing automation software.

Can it work while I am away?

That is the point of automation. If a system needs you to approve every individual action, you have mostly rebuilt manual trading with extra notifications.

A useful automation system lets you authorize a bounded piece of ongoing work. Individual actions can then happen inside that scope without repeatedly asking you the same question. Material changes should return to you when new authority is needed.

This is the model Chilla is built around: tell Chilla what you are working towards, review the work it prepares, authorize the material configuration, then let Chilla work within those boundaries and report back.

What happens when the AI is wrong?

Every serious answer should begin with the assumption that software can be wrong.

Markets can move unpredictably. A provider can reject an action. A network can disconnect. Data can arrive late. A model can misunderstand what you meant. A strategy that worked in one period can perform poorly in another.

So useful protections are less glamorous than “AI accuracy.” They include explicit authorization, bounded account scope, deterministic checks, provider acknowledgement, reconciliation, idempotency around retries, direct pause/stop controls and a factual activity record.

Those mechanisms do not guarantee profit. They reduce the number of ways ordinary software failures can quietly become financial authority.

Should I use a trading bot instead?

Possibly.

A fixed bot can be a better fit if you already know the exact rules you want to automate and you are comfortable configuring or maintaining them. Copy trading can be a better fit if your explicit goal is to mirror another trader. Manual trading can be a better fit if you want to make every discretionary decision yourself.

Chilla is aimed at a different experience: the user starts with the goal rather than assembling the workflow. Chilla works towards that goal using supported styles and markets, within the boundaries the user approves, and reports back.

What should I check before letting any AI trade through my account?

  • Where do my funds stay?
  • What exact authority am I granting?
  • Can the AI expand that authority by itself?
  • Can I inspect the material plan before it goes live?
  • Can I pause or stop work without relying on a conversation model?
  • What happens if the provider rejects an action or the connection drops?
  • Is there a factual record separate from the AI’s explanation?
  • Does the product promise outcomes it cannot guarantee?

If a product cannot answer those questions clearly, the sophistication of its model is not the main problem.

So: can AI trade for you?

Yes. The useful version of the question is whether you want software to give you ideas, follow fixed rules, copy somebody else, or carry out bounded ongoing work on your behalf.
