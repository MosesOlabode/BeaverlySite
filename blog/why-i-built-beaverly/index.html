Founder story

Why I built Beaverly

I started from inside the problem: years around financial markets, too much work living on screens, and a growing conviction that software should be able to carry more of the burden.

By Moses OlabodeJanuary 15, 2025Updated September 1, 2026

I did not begin Beaverly because I wanted to build an “AI startup.” I began with a much more practical frustration: financial-market work demanded too much operating from the person trying to use it.

I knew that experience personally. Trading had become a major part of my life, but the work around it was exhausting: watching markets, managing decisions, staying disciplined, dealing with tools that assumed the user wanted to become a full-time operator.

I kept coming back to one question: how much of this work could dependable software carry without taking control away from the person using it?

I first tried to hand the problem off

I was not a software engineer when this started. I hired developers and tried to turn the idea into a product through other people. Some of those attempts stalled or failed. Eventually the cost of waiting became higher than the cost of learning.

So I started building.

AI tools became part of that learning process. They helped me understand unfamiliar code, debug, compare approaches, and move faster. But the useful lesson was never “AI can build everything for you.” It was that implementation had become cheaper while judgment, architecture, testing, and knowing what should exist remained very human responsibilities.

The product kept teaching me what the company actually needed to become.

The first versions were much more mechanical

Early Chilla asked users to operate the product: connect an account, choose styles, choose markets, set boundaries, activate work, then inspect the results.

That was already useful automation, but it still left too much complexity with the user. We had moved work out of the market terminal and into a nicer interface, but the user was still operating the machinery.

The direction became clearer over time: the product should start with the person’s goal, not with the configuration needed to pursue it.

That is how Chilla became a financial worker

Today the product idea is much simpler to explain: tell Chilla what you are working towards. Chilla can reason over the supported ways it can work, prepare something concrete, let you inspect and change it, and ask for explicit approval before material live work becomes active.

Then it works and reports back.

That is a very different relationship from a dashboard, a trading bot, or an AI chat that happens to have a broker tool attached.

Why M-II exists separately

Making Chilla easier to talk to should not make financial execution less rigorous. That is why the conversational intelligence and the execution machinery are separate.

M-II is the engineered system underneath authorized live work. It owns the deterministic responsibilities that should not depend on a language model improvising correctly: authorization, validation, account scope, provider communication, lifecycle state, reconciliation, and traceability.

You talk to Chilla. M-II handles the machinery.

The company thesis became larger than trading UX

The more Beaverly developed, the more obvious the underlying mission became. People should not have to become operators of increasingly complicated software just because software became more powerful.

Good software should absorb complexity. It should let the person express intent, keep important control explicit, do the repeatable work dependably, and surface the parts that deserve attention.

Financial markets are where Beaverly is applying that idea first.

Move financial work from something people operate to something software can carry.

What has not changed

The core motivation is still freedom from unnecessary operating work. Chilla should not demand that someone stare at charts all day to prove they are taking their finances seriously. It also should not hide risk, promise outcomes, or make important choices disappear behind “AI.”

The standard is harder than that: make the experience calmer while the systems underneath become more disciplined.

That is what I am still building.
