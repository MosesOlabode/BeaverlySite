Founder story

Why I built Beaverly

I started from inside the problem: years around financial markets, too much work living on screens, failed attempts to hand the problem off, and eventually a simple conclusion — if this was going to exist, I had to build regardless.

By Moses OlabodeJanuary 15, 2025Updated September 2, 2026

I did not begin Beaverly because I wanted to build an “AI startup.” I began with a much more practical frustration: financial-market work demanded too much operating from the person trying to use it.

I knew that experience personally. Trading had become a major part of my life, but the work around it was exhausting: watching markets, managing decisions, staying disciplined, and dealing with tools that assumed the user wanted to become a full-time operator.

I kept coming back to one question: how much of this work could dependable software carry without taking control away from the person using it?

I first tried to hand the problem off

I was not a software engineer when this started. I hired developers and tried to turn the idea into a product through other people. Some disappeared. Some attempts stalled. Some work came back incomplete. I also knew people who could have materially changed the situation, and more than once I built plans around help that never actually arrived.

Eventually I stopped building plans around rescue.

If Beaverly was going to exist, I had to build with no permission. Build regardless.

The conditions were not romantic

At different points while building Beaverly, I sold four phones.

The first was my iPhone 15. Later I bought and sold an iPhone XR three different times as money tightened again. For more than a year, I had no phone of my own at all. If I needed to post, reply to someone, or use my banking apps, I borrowed my wife’s phone.

My development machine was a Samsung NP300E5A-A0KUK from 2012. The battery had failed, so it was basically a keyboard attached to a television: remove power and everything died. The LCD was worn out and flickered badly enough that the screen could freeze every few seconds. Some keyboard keys and touchpad buttons barely responded.

And I was building in Nigeria.

Electricity became part of the development stack.

There were days when no light meant no meaningful work and my mood would crash because I could see exactly what needed to be done but could not do it. Then electricity would return at midnight and I would be genuinely happy because I had another window to ship.

So I would.

Midnight became 3am. 3am became 7am. I would sleep, wake around 10am or 1pm, and continue. Eventually I got sick from the grind.

Then I went back to building.

Not “budget tight” broke. Sell-my-phone-four-times broke.

I became a post-AI systems engineer by force

I was not an engineer when I started Beaverly. I am now.

I became a post-AI systems engineer by force.

AI changed what was possible for me. It became an implementation layer: a way to generate code, understand unfamiliar systems, debug faster, compare approaches, and move through areas I had never been formally trained in.

But production does not care whether the code came from a human hand or a model. When account state is wrong, a provider disconnects, authentication breaks, a deployment regresses something unrelated, an execution needs reconciliation, a database truth disagrees with the UI, or an AI confidently proposes an architecture that does not fit the system, somebody still has to understand what is happening.

Shipping Beaverly forced me into the unglamorous edges of engineering: service boundaries, state ownership, databases, authentication, provider integrations, execution lifecycle, retries, reconciliation, infrastructure, observability, security boundaries, model failure modes, deployment regressions, and the discipline of proving that a fix actually fixed the original failure without quietly breaking something else.

The test was never whether I could reproduce every line of syntax from memory. The test was whether I understood the system well enough to make it work in the wild.

Over time, I did.

I was not completely alone

One person who kept standing with me was Kolawole, my founding partner.

There was a point where I could no longer split myself cleanly between earning money elsewhere and building Beaverly. Kola worked, supported me with cash when I could not afford to stop and earn, and also helped with cybersecurity work so the systems we were shipping were not merely functional, but safer in the wild.

That mattered. Especially in a period where a lot of other expected help simply did not materialize.

The product kept teaching me what it needed to become

The first versions of Chilla were much more mechanical. Users connected an account, chose styles, chose markets, set boundaries, activated work, then inspected the results.

That was already useful automation, but it still left too much complexity with the user. We had moved work out of the market terminal and into a better interface, but the user was still operating the machinery.

The direction became clearer over time: the product should start with the person’s goal, not with the configuration needed to pursue it.

That is how Chilla became a financial worker

Today the product idea is much simpler to explain: tell Chilla what you are working towards. Chilla can reason over the supported ways it can work, prepare something concrete, let you inspect and change it, and ask for explicit approval before material live work becomes active.

Then it works and reports back.

That is a very different relationship from a dashboard, a trading bot, or an AI chat that happens to have a broker tool attached.

Why M-II exists separately

Making Chilla easier to talk to should not make financial execution less rigorous. That is why the conversational intelligence and the execution machinery are separate.

M-II is the engineered system underneath authorized live work. It owns the deterministic responsibilities that should not depend on a language model improvising correctly: authorization, validation, account scope, provider communication, lifecycle state, reconciliation, and traceability.

You talk to Chilla. M-II handles the machinery.

The lesson became bigger than the product

The more Beaverly developed, the more obvious the underlying mission became. People should not have to become operators of increasingly complicated software just because software became more powerful.

Good software should absorb complexity. It should let the person express intent, keep important control explicit, do the repeatable work dependably, and surface the parts that deserve attention.

Financial markets are where Beaverly is applying that idea first.

Move financial work from something people operate to something software can carry.

What has not changed

The core motivation is still freedom from unnecessary operating work. Chilla should not demand that someone stare at charts all day to prove they are taking their finances seriously. It also should not hide risk, promise outcomes, or make important choices disappear behind “AI.”

The standard is harder than that: make the experience calmer while the systems underneath become more disciplined.

Beaverly did not come out of ideal conditions, a perfect machine, reliable electricity, a large engineering team, or people lining up to give me permission.

It came out of repeatedly losing the conditions I thought I needed and building anyway.

That is what I am still doing.
